\title{Where, Whether and When: Full-Duplex Human--Robot Collaboration}

\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) have made robots fluent conversationalists, but being a good collaborator takes more than knowing what to say. During a shared task, people continuously coordinate their speech, attention and actions: they notice when a partner needs help, know when to step in or stay silent, and adapt as the interaction unfolds. Most LLM-based robots still operate in discrete turns, which keeps them out of this coordination. My research explores how full-duplex models can let robots listen, speak and act in one continuous loop. The idea is to learn, at every moment, where the robot should direct its attention and whether it should speak or act, so that \emph{when} it does so emerges from these decisions. Using a collaborative board game, I have built a human--robot interaction corpus and multimodal self-reported enjoyment prediction. Next, I will ground full-duplex models in the shared task, align their behavior with human collaborators, and adapt it to each partner's experience.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Robots are starting to work alongside people on shared tasks, from assembling furniture to playing games or supporting care. In such tasks, being a good teammate~\cite{akata2020research} depends less on what is said than on how contributions are coordinated. People talk while they act. They look at the object they are referring to, glance at their partner to check understanding, step in when the partner hesitates, and stay quiet while the partner concentrates~\cite{skantze2021turn}. These signals run in parallel, often overlap, and are timed to the task as much as to the conversation.

Human--robot interaction (HRI) research has taught us much about how people respond to robots, but mostly with robots whose behaviour was scripted or remotely controlled. Of the studies presented at HRI 2013--2015, only 40\% used a mostly autonomous robot, while around as many relied on Wizard-of-Oz control or teleoperation~\cite{baxter2016characterising,riek2012wizard,clabaugh2019escaping}. Studies have served mainly to evaluate how people perceive and respond to robots, rather than to collect data from which robots can learn to interact better; data-driven social robotics is still emerging~\cite{lemaignan2018pinsoro,senft2019teaching}. And robots are usually designed once and deployed with fixed behaviour, although long-term interaction calls for continual learning and adaptation to each user~\cite{leite2013social,churamani2020continual,irfan2021lifelong}.

LLMs have begun to change this, letting robots decide autonomously \emph{what} to say. But they are built around turns. The robot waits until the person stops talking, generates a complete reply, and then performs it. When gaze and gestures are added, they are attached to the reply as tags, decided once per turn~\cite{lee2026recipe}. As a result, the robot cannot react while its partner is speaking or acting, cannot shift attention mid-sentence, and times its contributions by silence rather than by what is happening in the task (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, top).

Full-duplex spoken language models~\cite{defossez2024moshi,roy2026personaplex} offer a way out. They listen and speak at the same time, deciding every few tens of milliseconds. So far, however, they only handle speech. They do not see the shared task or where the partner is looking, they do not control the robot's gaze or actions, and they speak mainly when addressed or after silence~\cite{peng2026full}.

My thesis extends full-duplex models so that robots can coordinate in collaboration, reversing the three patterns above: the robot's behaviour is autonomous and learned rather than scripted, studies with people become training data rather than only evaluations, and the robot keeps adapting to each partner after deployment. The central idea is that, at every timestep, the robot makes two decisions: \emph{where} to direct its attention, and \emph{whether} to speak, act or wait. \emph{When} to contribute is then not a separate decision; it is the moment at which \emph{whether} turns to yes (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, bottom). Three questions follow:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{RQ1 (grounding):} How can a full-duplex model perceive the shared task and the partner's gaze, and control the robot's gaze and actions alongside its speech?
 \item \textbf{RQ2 (aligning):} How can its decisions be aligned with how people coordinate in the task, when only a small corpus of human behaviour exists?
 \item \textbf{RQ3 (adapting):} How can it adapt to what each partner experiences, such as their enjoyment, which cannot be measured directly during the interaction?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Turn-taking} People exchange turns with gaps of around 200\,ms, using cues from prosody to gaze and task state~\cite{skantze2021turn}. Voice Activity Projection learns to predict turns from dialogue audio~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} and reduces delays and interruptions on robots~\cite{skantze2025applying}, but remains a separate module that tells a turn-based system when to speak.

\paragraph{Full-duplex and multimodal models} Full-duplex models remove the turn structure~\cite{defossez2024moshi} and are beginning to emit more than speech, such as tool calls~\cite{zhang2026duplexsla} or facial motion~\cite{jiang2026moshiface}. Robot gaze regulates the floor and joint attention~\cite{admoni2017social}, and game agents can ground their dialogue in a planning engine~\cite{bakhtin2022cicero}. No full-duplex model yet combines these: perceiving a shared task and the partner's gaze, and acting on both.

\paragraph{Learning from people} With large datasets, models can be fine-tuned with adapters~\cite{hu2022lora}; with small ones, a frozen model can be steered instead~\cite{yang2021fudge,rimsky2024caa}. Like the reward models of reinforcement learning from human feedback~\cite{christiano2017deep}, predictors of partner states such as enjoyment~\cite{pereira2024multimodal} can turn human signals into feedback.

\section{What I Have Done}

\paragraph{A collaborative testbed and corpus} I co-developed an open platform in which a participant and a Furhat robot~\cite{al2012furhat} play the cooperative board game \emph{Pandemic} on a shared touchscreen, with an inspectable game engine and an LLM-based dialogue system~\cite{anon2026platform}. With it, we collected a corpus of 70 interactions with 36 participants, with synchronised audio, video, gaze, transcripts, game logs and questionnaires~\cite{anon2026dataset}. This corpus shows how people coordinate speech, gaze and moves in the task.

\paragraph{Predicting enjoyment} Enjoyment is usually measured only by asking people afterwards. I showed that self-reported enjoyment can be predicted in real time from speech, more accurately than an experienced HRI annotator~\cite{anon2025joy}. Such a predictor lets a robot estimate how its partner feels without asking.

\paragraph{Timing of a full-duplex model} In a pilot on my corpus, I let an off-the-shelf full-duplex model take the place of a participant. It proposed 3.5 times as many speech onsets as the person it replaced, yet matched only a quarter of theirs. However, a small classifier reading the model's internal states predicted the human's next onset well (AUC 0.87, against 0.51 for the model's own decision). The model already encodes \emph{when} a person would speak; it simply does not act on it. This motivates aligning its decisions with human behaviour rather than retraining it from scratch.

\section{What I Will Do}

\paragraph{Aim 1: Ground the model in the task (RQ1)} I will add the game state and the partner's gaze as input streams to a full-duplex model, and robot gaze and game actions as output streams, all on the model's clock. The game state comes from a game-playing agent that I have trained through self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero}, which already outperforms the platform's rule-based player. Gaze is learned first from large conversational datasets~\cite{agrawal2025seamless} and then refined on my corpus. The result is a \emph{general task robot} that behaves as any competent partner would.

\paragraph{Aim 2: Align with human collaborators (RQ2)} My corpus is too small to fine-tune a large model on, so I will extend the pilot classifier into a small steering module that sits on top of the frozen model and nudges its decisions towards what people did: when they spoke, where they looked and when they moved. The result is a \emph{task-specific robot}.

\paragraph{Aim 3: Adapt to each partner (RQ3)} The enjoyment predictor will serve as a reward, much like the reward model in reinforcement learning from human feedback. Fine-tuning against it lets the robot adapt to the partner in front of it, for example by interrupting less when predicted enjoyment drops. The result is a \emph{partner-specific robot}.

\paragraph{Evaluation} After each aim, new participants will play with the robot, compared against the turn-based baseline and the previous stage. I will measure where the robot looks, its interruptions and gaps, and team performance, and ask about enjoyment, workload and the robot's competence. Because Aim 3 optimises \emph{predicted} enjoyment, it is judged by what participants actually report.

\section{Conclusion}

My thesis aims to move robots beyond taking turns, towards partners that coordinate with people in real time, deciding at every moment where to look and whether to speak or act. With a testbed, a corpus and first results in place, I am now building the model itself. At HRI Pioneers, I would value feedback on how to evaluate real-time coordination, how to design studies that capture it, and how robots should adapt to people's inferred internal states.

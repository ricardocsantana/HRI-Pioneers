\title{Where, When, and Whether: Full-Duplex Robot Teammates Grounded in the Task, Steered by the Partner}

\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) have made robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. Collaboration also demands knowing \emph{where} to direct attention, \emph{when} to speak, and \emph{whether} to act at all. Where depends on what a robot perceives (the partner's gaze, the task state); when and whether depend on the partner's internal state, revealed by signals such as enjoyment or brain activity that are measurable only in studies. I built a collaborative-game testbed and corpus, and models that predict self-reported and neural signals from interaction data. I now adapt an open full-duplex Spoken Language Model in two stages: fine-tuning it on abundant task and gaze data to ground where it looks and acts, then steering it, supervised by predicted partner state, to decide when and whether to act. The aim is robots that adapt to each partner rather than expecting the opposite.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have made robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. Teammates that augment rather than replace people~\cite{akata2020research} must also know \emph{where} to direct attention, \emph{when} to speak or act, and \emph{whether} to act at all. People regulate these with gaze, timing, and the state of the shared task~\cite{skantze2021turn}, cues that LLM-based agents neither perceive nor produce.

These skills draw on two kinds of information. \emph{Where} to look and act depends on what the robot perceives at every moment: the partner's speech and gaze, and the task state. These streams exist at test time and in abundance: a game engine generates unlimited self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero}, and conversational gaze is learnable from large corpora~\cite{agrawal2025seamless}. I add them as input and output channels and fine-tune a full-duplex model on them (Stage~1). \emph{When} and \emph{whether} depend on the partner's internal state, revealed by signals such as enjoyment or brain activity that exist only in studies, never at deployment (\emph{privileged information}~\cite{vapnik2009lupi}), and in small amounts~\cite{anon2026dataset}. I train predictors that recover these signals from interaction data and use them to \emph{steer} the frozen model through a small learned intervention (Stage~2; Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). My overarching question is: \emph{how can a robot adapt its attention, timing, and interventions to a human teammate, using observable interaction cues and predictions of the partner's internal state?}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{RQ1 (\emph{where}):} Can task state and partner gaze, as input streams and a gaze output channel, ground a full-duplex model's decisions in the shared task?
 \item \textbf{RQ2 (\emph{when}):} Can the frozen model be steered, from one study's data, to take turns at human-like moments?
 \item \textbf{RQ3 (\emph{whether}):} Can predictors of partner state, trained where self-reports and brain data exist, decide whether the robot acts, without measuring those signals at test time?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Grounding attention and action (where)}
Gaze regulates the floor and joint attention~\cite{admoni2017social}, and responsive robot gaze raises social presence~\cite{pereira2019responsive}. Multimodal LLMs steer robot gaze through tool calls but decide once per turn~\cite{lee2026recipe}. Open full-duplex models~\cite{defossez2024moshi,roy2026personaplex} decide every 80\,ms frame and are gaining channels beyond speech: tool calls co-decoded with speech on one clock~\cite{zhang2026duplexsla,balam2026nemotronvoicechat}, and facial motion generated alongside it~\cite{jiang2026moshiface}. Game agents ground their communication in a policy-value network~\cite{kramar2022negotiation,bakhtin2022cicero}, and projecting an engine's hidden state into an LLM as tokens outperforms describing it in text~\cite{si2026exploring}. No full-duplex model yet perceives a shared task or its partner's gaze; I add both as inputs, and robot gaze as an output.

\paragraph{Coordinating turns (when)}
People exchange turns with gaps of around 200\,ms, projected from cues ranging from syntax and prosody to gaze and task state~\cite{skantze2021turn}. Predictive models learn it from data, TurnGPT~\cite{ekstedt2020turngpt} from text and Voice Activity Projection (VAP)~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} from dyadic audio, and VAP has reduced delays and interruptions on an LLM-driven Furhat~\cite{skantze2025applying}. I instead steer the speaking model's own turn decisions toward human timing.

\paragraph{Adapting from scarce partner signals (whether)}
Full-duplex models speak when addressed or at silence; even when handed the floor, they rarely correct a false claim or warn of a hazard~\cite{peng2026full}. Human signals are too costly to supervise training directly, so Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) optimizes against a reward model fit to them~\cite{christiano2017deep,ouyang2022rlhf}. Signals of whether acting helps \emph{this} partner are scarcer still, yet predictable from interaction data, from self-reported and annotated enjoyment~\cite{pereira2024multimodal,anon2025joy} to the partner's brain responses~\cite{zada2026linguistic,anon2026fmri}. Prompts shape \emph{what} more than \emph{when}, and weight updates~\cite{hu2022lora,rafailov2023dpo} need more data than HRI provides. Activation steering~\cite{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,zou2023repe} is data-efficient and corrects slow yielding in full-duplex models~\cite{chang2026overcoming}, but can also shift content, whereas logit steering~\cite{yang2021fudge,krause2021gedi} reweights only the next token. I supervise a logit-level intervention with predicted partner state.

\section{Prior Work: Testbed and Partner-State Predictors}

\paragraph{Testbed and observed behaviour}
I co-developed an open platform in which a participant and a Furhat robot~\cite{al2012furhat} play the cooperative board game \emph{Pandemic} on a shared touchscreen, with an inspectable game engine, a rule-based solver playing the robot's side, and an LLM-based grounded dialogue system~\cite{anon2026platform}. With it, we collected a corpus of 70 interactions with 36 participants, with a game-adaptive and a generic robot voice: audio, video, gaze and annotated transcripts, aligned with game logs and questionnaires~\cite{anon2026dataset}. Its human turn onsets are the \emph{observed} target for Stage~2a.

\paragraph{Self-reported experience}
Enjoyment exists only in studies, reported once, afterwards. My Speech-to-Joy predicts it in real time from speech and text embeddings, outperforming an experienced HRI annotator and LLM baselines, even from audio alone~\cite{anon2025joy}. Retrained on my corpus's per-game ratings, it becomes a \emph{self-reported} partner-state signal (Stage~2b).

\paragraph{Measured signals}
Brain activity exists only in the scanner, yet it can index whether a partner comprehends the robot and models its mind. I adapted a multimodal whole-brain fMRI foundation model to predict it from live conversation, improving held-out prediction by 15\% over a ridge encoder~\cite{anon2026brainconv}. Across two fMRI corpora with human and robot partners (70 participants), transfer between partners was asymmetric where the robot's visual behaviour differed most from a human's~\cite{anon2026brainhri}, so such predictors are sensitive to robot behaviour.

\section{Current and Future Work: Model Adaptation}
\label{sec:current}

\paragraph{Stage 1: grounding where (RQ1)}
On a JAX port of the game, a policy--value network trained by imitation and then Gumbel AlphaZero self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero,danihelka2022gumbel} wins 71\% of games at rulebook difficulty, against 24\% for the solver; its last hidden layer can serve as the task-state stream. For gaze, I plan to add partner gaze as input and a head emitting quantised gaze tokens alongside speech~\cite{jiang2026moshiface,mentzer2024fsq}. In the corpus, the robot looking at the participant predicts their next onset a second ahead: where the robot looks changes when the partner speaks.

\paragraph{Stage 2a: steering when (RQ2, pilot)}
Off the shelf, the model proposed 3.5$\times$ as many speech onsets as the participant it replaced, yet matched only a quarter of theirs ($\pm$960\,ms). Prompt rewrites did not change when it acted, and vetoes after the argmax failed: the model re-proposed, and denying every proposal silenced it for good. Interventions before the argmax worked, and the hidden state appears to know more than the head: a small recurrent head over $h_t$ predicted the partner's next onset with AUC 0.87 (leave-one-participant-out, 18 participants), against 0.51 for the model's own start-versus-wait margin, doubling onset recall at the same false-start rate. The head is backbone-agnostic, so it is retrained after Stage~1 to steer gaze too.

\paragraph{Stage 2b: steering whether (RQ3)}
Whether to act has two sources: the task value $v$ says whether acting helps the game, predicted partner state whether it helps this partner. I will supervise the head with predicted \emph{self-reported} and \emph{measured} signals, as targets that calibrate the bias per partner and moment, or as rewards for a contextual bandit over a few logits, offline on the corpus and then online. The override failures set a safety constraint: bias, but never deny every turn.

\paragraph{Evaluation}
Each stage is tested offline on the corpus, then with new participants against an unadapted robot. \emph{Where} (RQ1): robot gaze to the partner and to the task element under discussion, against human gaze, and win rate. \emph{When} (RQ2): onset latency relative to human onsets, and interruption rate. \emph{Whether} (RQ3): missed opportunities (the robot stays silent as $v$ drops), and whether its interventions precede better moves. Ratings of responsiveness, workload, and enjoyment test whether these gains matter to partners.

\title{Where and Whether: What Conversational Robots Need to Collaborate}

\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) have made conversational robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. To collaborate, they also need to decide, at every moment, \emph{where} to direct attention and \emph{whether} to speak or act; \emph{when} they act then follows. Each decision builds on what any competent partner would do, which can be learned from abundant data available at test time: the partner's speech and gaze, and the state of the shared task. Adapting it to how people play this task, and to what this partner needs, relies on scarce data: one corpus, and internal states such as enjoyment that can be collected only in studies. I built a collaborative-game testbed and corpus, and a model that predicts self-reported enjoyment from speech alone. I now adapt a full-duplex Spoken Language Model in three stages: into a general task robot, fine-tuned on task and gaze data; a task-specific robot, imitating what people do; and a partner-specific robot, rewarded by what each partner is predicted to experience. The aim is robots that adapt to each person rather than expecting people to adapt to them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have made conversational robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. To collaborate as teammates that augment rather than replace people~\cite{akata2020research}, they also need to decide, at every moment, \emph{where} to direct attention and \emph{whether} to speak or act. \emph{When} needs no separate decision: it is the moment \emph{whether} turns to yes. People regulate both with gaze, timing, and the state of the shared task~\cite{skantze2021turn}, cues that LLM-based robots neither perceive nor produce.

I build these decisions up in three stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). A \emph{general task robot} does what any competent partner would, from what it perceives at every moment: the partner's speech and gaze, and the task state. These streams exist at test time and in abundance: conversational gaze is learnable from large corpora~\cite{agrawal2025seamless}, and a game engine generates unlimited self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero}. I add them as input and output channels of a full-duplex model (already proficient in conversational speech) and fine-tune it on them (Stage~1). Stage~2 \emph{aligns} the model with people, where data is scarce. How people play this task is recorded in a single corpus~\cite{anon2026dataset}, too little to fine-tune on, so a small intervention learns from it by steering the frozen model's logits, yielding a \emph{task-specific robot} (Stage~2a). What this partner needs depends on their internal state, revealed by signals such as self-reported enjoyment that exist only in studies, never at test time (\emph{privileged information}~\cite{vapnik2009lupi}). A predictor I train to recover partner state from interaction data acts as a learned theory of mind: like the reward model in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), it turns these scarce signals into feedback that yields a \emph{partner-specific robot} (Stage~2b).
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{RQ1 (predicting, prior work):} Can partner state that exists only in studies, such as self-reported enjoyment, be predicted from interaction data alone?
 \item \textbf{RQ2 (grounding):} How can task state and partner gaze be incorporated into a full-duplex model, as input streams on its 80 ms clock with robot gaze as an output, so that where it looks and whether it acts, and hence when, match human behaviour in the shared task?
 \item \textbf{RQ3 (adapting):} Can predicted partner state, learned where self-reports exist, serve as reinforcement feedback that adapts where and whether to each partner without measuring those signals at test time?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Coordinating turns}
People exchange turns with gaps of around 200\,ms, projected from cues ranging from syntax and prosody to gaze and task state~\cite{skantze2021turn}. Predictive models learn it from data, TurnGPT~\cite{ekstedt2020turngpt} from text and Voice Activity Projection (VAP)~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} from dyadic audio, and VAP has reduced delays and interruptions on an LLM-driven Furhat~\cite{skantze2025applying}. I instead align the speaking model's own turn decisions with human timing.

\paragraph{Grounding attention and action}
Gaze regulates the floor and joint attention~\cite{admoni2017social}, and responsive robot gaze raises social presence~\cite{pereira2019responsive}. Multimodal LLMs steer robot gaze through tool calls but decide once per turn~\cite{lee2026recipe}. Open full-duplex models~\cite{defossez2024moshi,roy2026personaplex} decide every 80\,ms frame and are gaining channels beyond speech: tool calls co-decoded with speech on one clock~\cite{zhang2026duplexsla,balam2026nemotronvoicechat}, and facial motion generated alongside it~\cite{jiang2026moshiface}. Game agents ground their communication in a policy-value network~\cite{kramar2022negotiation,bakhtin2022cicero}, and projecting an engine's hidden state into an LLM as tokens outperforms describing it in text~\cite{si2026exploring}. No full-duplex model yet perceives a shared task or its partner's gaze; I add both as inputs, and robot gaze as an output.

\paragraph{Adapting from abundant and scarce data}
Full-duplex models speak when addressed or at silence, rarely when the situation calls for it~\cite{peng2026full}. With abundant data, parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA~\cite{houlsby2019adapters,han2024parameter,hu2022lora} adds inputs and outputs cheaply (Stage~1). Scarce data, like human behaviour in one task, calls for steering a frozen model: activation steering~\cite{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,chang2026overcoming} is data-efficient but can shift content; logit steering~\cite{yang2021fudge,krause2021gedi} reweights only the next token (Stage~2a). Scarce data can also train a reward model, as RLHF does from human judgments~\cite{christiano2017deep}; likewise, partner state such as self-reported enjoyment is predictable from interaction data~\cite{pereira2024multimodal,anon2025joy}, so such a predictor, a learned theory of mind, can serve as reward (Stage~2b).

\section{Prior Work: Testbed and Partner Predictor}

\paragraph{Testbed and observed behaviour}
I co-developed an open platform in which a participant and a Furhat robot~\cite{al2012furhat} play the cooperative board game \emph{Pandemic} on a shared touchscreen, with an inspectable game engine, a rule-based solver playing the robot's side, and an LLM-based grounded dialogue system~\cite{anon2026platform}. With it, we collected a corpus of 70 interactions with 36 participants, with a game-adaptive and a generic robot voice: audio, video, gaze and annotated transcripts, aligned with game logs and questionnaires~\cite{anon2026dataset}. Its human turn onsets and gaze are the \emph{observed} targets for Stage~2a.

\paragraph{Self-reported experience (RQ1)}
Enjoyment exists only in studies, reported or annotated once, afterwards. My Speech-to-Joy predicts self-reports in real time from speech and text embeddings, outperforming an experienced HRI annotator and LLM baselines, even from audio alone~\cite{anon2025joy}. Retrained on my corpus's per-game ratings, it becomes the partner-state reward for Stage~2b.

\section{Current and Future Work: Model Adaptation}
\label{sec:current}

\paragraph{Stage 1: general task robot (RQ2)}
On a JAX port of the game, a policy-value network trained by imitation and then Gumbel AlphaZero self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero,danihelka2022gumbel} wins 71\% of games at rulebook difficulty, against 24\% for the rule-based solver; its last hidden layer can serve as the task-state stream, and its value $v$ as a signal of how certain the robot is. For gaze, I plan to add partner gaze as input and a head emitting quantised gaze tokens alongside speech~\cite{jiang2026moshiface,mentzer2024fsq}, by first training on large dyadic conversational corpora and then refining on the board-game corpus.

\paragraph{Stage 2a: task-specific robot via logit steering (RQ2, pilot)}
Off the shelf, the model proposed 3.5$\times$ as many speech onsets as the participant it replaced, yet matched only a quarter of theirs ($\pm$960\,ms). In preliminary experiments, a small recurrent head over $h_t$ predicted the partner's next onset with AUC 0.87 (leave-one-participant-out, 18 participants), against 0.51 for the model's own start-versus-wait margin, doubling onset recall at the same false-start rate. Turn onsets are the first target; the head is backbone-agnostic, so after Stage~1 it is retrained to imitate human gaze and actions too.

\paragraph{Stage 2b: partner-specific robot (RQ3)}
The enjoyment predictor acts as a learned theory of mind and, like RLHF's reward model, provides reinforcement feedback: the model is fine-tuned against it, with a KL penalty toward the Stage~2a model, to adapt each decision to the partner (e.g., speaking less when predicted enjoyment drops after interruptions), not just the game.

\section{Evaluation}
Each stage is evaluated offline on the corpus, then with new participants against the unadapted model, so that each gain is attributed to a stage (1, 2a, 2b). Objectively (RQ2), I compare win rate, and the robot's behaviour distribution with that of human players of different skill levels: gaze to the partner and to the task element under discussion (\emph{where}), acting or staying silent as $v$ drops (\emph{whether}), and the timing this produces, onset latency and interruptions (\emph{when}). Subjectively (RQ3), partners report enjoyment, cognitive load and robot competence. Since Stage~2b optimises \emph{predicted} partner state, these reports, not the predictors, test it, which keeps the evaluation from being circular.

\section{Conclusion}
To collaborate, robots must decide at every moment where to look and whether to act. My thesis builds these decisions from abundant to scarce data: a general task robot, a task-specific robot that imitates how people play, and a partner-specific robot rewarded by predicted partner experience. As predictors stand in for self-reports, the robot keeps adapting to each partner beyond the study, adapting to people rather than expecting people to adapt to it.

\title{Where, When, and Whether: What Conversational Robots Need to Collaborate}

\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) have made conversational robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. To collaborate, they also need to decide \emph{where} to direct attention, \emph{when} to speak, and \emph{whether} to act at all. Each decision has a generic part, what any good partner would do, and a partner-adaptive part, what this partner needs. The general part can be learned from abundant data available at test time: the partner's speech and gaze, and the state of the shared task. The partner-specific part depends on internal states, such as enjoyment or mental state, that can be collected only in studies. I built a collaborative-game testbed and corpus, and models that recover these self-reported and neural signals from interaction data alone. I now adapt open full-duplex Spoken Language Models in two stages: fine-tuning on task and gaze data to ground all three decisions, then aligning it with people: imitating scarce task-specific behaviour through logit steering, and using predicted partner state as a learned theory of mind for reinforcement feedback. The aim is robots that adapt to each person rather than expecting people to adapt to them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have made conversational robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. To collaborate as teammates that augment rather than replace people~\cite{akata2020research}, they also need to know \emph{where} to direct attention, \emph{when} to speak or act, and \emph{whether} to act at all. People regulate these with gaze, timing, and the state of the shared task~\cite{skantze2021turn}, cues that LLM-based robots neither perceive nor produce.

Each decision has two parts. The \emph{general} part, what any good partner would do, depends on what the robot perceives at every moment: the partner's speech and gaze, and the task state. These streams exist at test time and in abundance: a game engine generates unlimited self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero}, and conversational gaze is learnable from large corpora~\cite{agrawal2025seamless}. I add them as input and output channels of a full-duplex model (already proficient in conversational speech) and fine-tune it on them (Stage~1). The \emph{partner-specific} part, what this partner needs, depends on their internal state, revealed by signals such as enjoyment or brain states. These exist only in studies and in small amounts~\cite{anon2026dataset}, never at test time (\emph{privileged information}~\cite{vapnik2009lupi}). Stage~2 \emph{aligns} the model with people in two separate ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). First, human behaviour in this task is recorded in a single study, too little to fine-tune on, so a small intervention imitates it by steering the frozen model's logits. Second, predictors I train to recover partner state from interaction data act as a learned theory of mind: like the reward model in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), they turn scarce human signals into feedback that adapts the model's speech, gaze and actions to each partner. My overarching question is: \emph{how can a conversational robot become a collaborator that adapts its attention, timing, and actions to its partner, using observable interaction cues and predictions of the partner's internal state?}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{RQ1 (predicting, prior work):} Can partner states that exist only in studies, self-reported enjoyment and measured brain activity, be predicted from interaction data alone?
 \item \textbf{RQ2 (grounding):} How can task state and partner gaze be incorporated into a full-duplex model, as input streams on its 80 ms clock with robot gaze as an output, so that where, when and whether it acts match human behaviour in the shared task?
 \item \textbf{RQ3 (adapting):} Can predicted partner state, learned where self-reports and brain data exist, serve as reinforcement feedback that adapts where, when and whether to each partner without measuring those signals at test time?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Coordinating turns}
People exchange turns with gaps of around 200\,ms, projected from cues ranging from syntax and prosody to gaze and task state~\cite{skantze2021turn}. Predictive models learn it from data, TurnGPT~\cite{ekstedt2020turngpt} from text and Voice Activity Projection (VAP)~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} from dyadic audio, and VAP has reduced delays and interruptions on an LLM-driven Furhat~\cite{skantze2025applying}. I instead align the speaking model's own turn decisions with human timing.

\paragraph{Grounding attention and action}
Gaze regulates the floor and joint attention~\cite{admoni2017social}, and responsive robot gaze raises social presence~\cite{pereira2019responsive}. Multimodal LLMs steer robot gaze through tool calls but decide once per turn~\cite{lee2026recipe}. Open full-duplex models~\cite{defossez2024moshi,roy2026personaplex} decide every 80\,ms frame and are gaining channels beyond speech: tool calls co-decoded with speech on one clock~\cite{zhang2026duplexsla,balam2026nemotronvoicechat}, and facial motion generated alongside it~\cite{jiang2026moshiface}. Game agents ground their communication in a policy-value network~\cite{kramar2022negotiation,bakhtin2022cicero}, and projecting an engine's hidden state into an LLM as tokens outperforms describing it in text~\cite{si2026exploring}. No full-duplex model yet perceives a shared task or its partner's gaze; I add both as inputs, and robot gaze as an output.

\paragraph{Adapting from abundant and scarce data}
Full-duplex models speak when addressed or at silence, rarely when the situation calls for it~\cite{peng2026full}. Where data is abundant, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)~\cite{houlsby2019adapters,han2024parameter} such as LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} adds new inputs and outputs cheaply (Stage~1). Task-specific human behaviour, however, is recorded only in studies; imitating it without fine-tuning calls for steering a frozen model: activation steering~\cite{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,chang2026overcoming} is data-efficient but can shift content; logit steering~\cite{yang2021fudge,krause2021gedi} reweights only the next token. A separate route aligns a model with what people experience rather than what they do: RLHF turns scarce human judgments into a reward model that provides reinforcement feedback~\cite{christiano2017deep}. Partner state is likewise predictable from interaction data, from self-reported enjoyment~\cite{pereira2024multimodal,anon2025joy} to brain responses~\cite{zada2026linguistic,anon2026brainconv}, so such predictors, a learned theory of mind of the partner, can provide that feedback.

\section{Prior Work: Testbed and Partner Predictors}

\paragraph{Testbed and observed behaviour}
I co-developed an open platform in which a participant and a Furhat robot~\cite{al2012furhat} play the cooperative board game \emph{Pandemic} on a shared touchscreen, with an inspectable game engine, a rule-based solver playing the robot's side, and an LLM-based grounded dialogue system~\cite{anon2026platform}. With it, we collected a corpus of 70 interactions with 36 participants, with a game-adaptive and a generic robot voice: audio, video, gaze and annotated transcripts, aligned with game logs and questionnaires~\cite{anon2026dataset}. Its human turn onsets and gaze are the \emph{observed} targets for Stage~2a.

\paragraph{Self-reported experience (RQ1)}
Enjoyment exists only in studies, reported once, afterwards. My Speech-to-Joy predicts it in real time from speech and text embeddings, outperforming an experienced HRI annotator and LLM baselines, even from audio alone~\cite{anon2025joy}. Retrained on my corpus's per-game ratings, it becomes a \emph{self-reported} partner-state signal (Stage~2b).

\paragraph{Measured signals (RQ1)}
Brain activity exists only in the scanner, yet it can index whether a partner comprehends the robot and models its mind. I adapted a multimodal whole-brain fMRI foundation model to predict it from live conversation, improving held-out prediction by 15\% over a ridge encoder~\cite{anon2026brainconv}. Across two fMRI corpora with human and robot partners (70 participants), transfer between partners was asymmetric where the robot's visual behaviour differed most from a human's~\cite{anon2026brainhri}, so such predictors are sensitive to robot behaviour.

\section{Current and Future Work: Model Adaptation}
\label{sec:current}

\paragraph{Stage 1: grounding (RQ2)}
On a JAX port of the game, a policy--value network trained by imitation and then Gumbel AlphaZero self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero,danihelka2022gumbel} wins 71\% of games at rulebook difficulty, against 24\% for the solver; its last hidden layer can serve as the task-state stream, and its value $v$ as a general signal for whether to act. For gaze, I plan to add partner gaze as input and a head emitting quantised gaze tokens alongside speech~\cite{jiang2026moshiface,mentzer2024fsq}. The decisions are coupled: in the corpus, the robot looking at the participant predicts their next onset a second ahead, so where the robot looks changes when the partner speaks, and one model should make all three.

\paragraph{Stage 2a: imitation via logit steering (RQ2, pilot)}
Off the shelf, the model proposed 3.5$\times$ as many speech onsets as the participant it replaced, yet matched only a quarter of theirs ($\pm$960\,ms). Prompt rewrites did not change when it acted, and vetoes after the argmax failed: the model re-proposed, and denying every proposal silenced it for good. Interventions before the argmax worked, and the hidden state appears to know more than the head: a small recurrent head over $h_t$ predicted the partner's next onset with AUC 0.87 (leave-one-participant-out, 18 participants), against 0.51 for the model's own start-versus-wait margin, doubling onset recall at the same false-start rate. Turn onsets are the first target; the head is backbone-agnostic, so after Stage~1 it is retrained to imitate human gaze and actions too.

\paragraph{Stage 2b: partner-state feedback (RQ3)}
Predictors of \emph{self-reported} and \emph{measured} signals act as a learned theory of mind and, like RLHF's reward model, provide reinforcement feedback: the model is fine-tuned against them, with a KL penalty toward the Stage~2a model, to adapt each decision to the partner: \emph{where}, raising mutual gaze when predicted comprehension drops; \emph{when}, waiting longer for a hesitant partner; and \emph{whether}, acting as $v$ drops only if it helps this partner, not just the game. Training runs offline on the corpus, then online. The pilot's override failures set a safety constraint: adapt timing, but never suppress every turn.

\section{Evaluation}
Each stage is tested offline on the corpus, then with new participants against an unadapted robot; each decision is measured after Stage~1 and again after Stage~2, so partner adaptation is tested as a gain over grounding alone. \emph{Where}: robot gaze to the partner and to the task element under discussion, against human gaze, and win rate. \emph{When}: onset latency relative to human onsets, and interruption rate. \emph{Whether}: missed opportunities (the robot stays silent as $v$ drops), and whether its interventions precede better moves. Ratings of responsiveness, workload, and enjoyment test whether these gains matter to partners.

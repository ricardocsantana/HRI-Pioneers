\title{When, Where, and Whether to Speak: Teaching Robots to Communicate as Teammates}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models have largely solved \emph{what} a robot should say; in embodied, task-oriented dialogue the harder questions are \emph{when}, \emph{where}, and \emph{whether} to say it. Full-duplex speech models listen and speak at once and act through tool calls, yet cannot see their partner or the task, and off the shelf talk too much and act too rarely. My research asks how LLM-based agents can gain the temporal, spatial, and social awareness that natural collaboration requires, matching each adaptation to the data available for it. I built a collaborative-game testbed and corpus, and supervision signals from observed behaviour to self-reported enjoyment and brain activity. Where data is abundant (game self-play, gaze), I fine-tune the model to perceive and produce task state and gaze; where it is scarce (human signals), I steer it. A first steering prototype, which reads an open full-duplex model's hidden state and biases its logits, doubles turn-onset recall offline at a fixed false-start rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have made robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. Natural collaboration also requires knowing \emph{when} to speak or act, \emph{where} to direct attention in the shared space, and \emph{whether} to speak at all given the partner's state. People regulate these with timing, gaze, and the state of the shared task~\cite{skantze2021turn}, cues that LLM-based agents neither perceive nor produce. My thesis asks: \emph{How can we empower LLM-based agents with the social, temporal, and spatial awareness necessary to facilitate natural human--AI task-oriented embodied dialogue?}

The data for this is lopsided. Task and gaze data are abundant: a game engine generates unlimited self-play, and gaze accompanies every recorded interaction. The signals that say whether an interaction worked for \emph{this} partner are scarce: an HRI study yields tens of participants and a few hours. I therefore match the intervention to the data, fine-tuning a full-duplex model where data is abundant and \emph{steering} it, through a small learned intervention on the frozen model, where it is scarce (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}):
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{RQ1 (temporal, \emph{when}):} Can a full-duplex model be steered, from one study's data, to take turns and act at human-like moments?
 \item \textbf{RQ2 (spatial, \emph{where}):} How can task state and gaze, as input and output, ground dialogue in the shared task space?
 \item \textbf{RQ3 (social, \emph{whether}):} Which human signals (observed, self-reported, measured) should supervise steering so the agent adapts to its partner?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Full-duplex and embodied models}
Cascaded ASR--LLM--TTS pipelines are turn-based and time their turns with silence thresholds. Lee et al.~\cite{lee2026recipe} pair a real-time multimodal LLM with tool calls, but on a commercial API whose decisions cannot be inspected. Open full-duplex models~\cite{defossez2024moshi} make those decisions explicit. My base model, NVIDIA NemotronLabs VoiceChat~\cite{nvidia2026voicechat}, is an open 11B full-duplex model and the first open one with tool calling: every 80\,ms it emits a token on a text channel (wait, start, or stop speaking) and on a function channel (call a tool or not). Embodied multimodal models ground language in perception~\cite{driess2023palme}, and CICERO couples a dialogue model to a strategic planner~\cite{bakhtin2022cicero}; neither handles the timing of face-to-face spoken collaboration. Following both, I add task state and gaze as streams of a full-duplex model.

\subsection{Adapting with little data}
Interventions differ in where they write, trading data needs against precision. \emph{Context} (prompts, injected state) needs no data but shapes \emph{what} is said more than \emph{when}. \emph{Soft embeddings}~\cite{lester2021prompt,li2021prefix} are learned but static; \emph{weight} updates~\cite{houlsby2019adapters,hu2022lora,ouyang2022rlhf,rafailov2023dpo} are expressive but need far more data than HRI provides. \emph{Activation} steering~\cite{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,zou2023repe,li2023iti} is data-efficient but shifts everything downstream, including content. \emph{Logit} steering~\cite{dathathri2020pplm,yang2021fudge,krause2021gedi} reweights the next-token distribution. I update weights only for new channels with abundant data. The decisions human signals should shape \emph{are} tokens, so for them I bias logits: the effect is bounded and auditable, and only a small readout must be learned.

\section{Prior Work: Testbed and Supervision}

\subsection{Testbed and observed behaviour}
I co-developed an open platform in which a participant and a Furhat robot~\cite{al2012furhat} play the cooperative board game \emph{Pandemic} on a shared touchscreen, with an inspectable game engine, a rule-based solver playing the robot's side, and an LLM dialogue manager~\cite{anon2026platform}. With it I collected 70 interactions with 36 participants: transcripts annotated for interaction failures, audio, facial action units, head pose and gaze, aligned with game events, robot logs, and questionnaires~\cite{anon2026dataset}. This is the \emph{observed} reference steering should match (part~2a).

\subsection{Self-reported experience}
Self-reports are ground truth for experience but arrive once, afterwards. Speech-to-Joy predicts post-interaction enjoyment from frozen speech embeddings, correlating significantly with self-reports and outperforming an experienced HRI annotator and LLM baselines while running in real time~\cite{anon2025joy}: an online proxy for a self-reported objective (part~2b).

\subsection{Measured signals}
I adapted a multimodal whole-brain fMRI foundation model to live conversation, improving held-out prediction by 15\% over a ridge encoder~\cite{anon2026brainconv}. In two human--robot fMRI corpora (70 participants), transfer between human and robot partners depended on multimodal dynamics, above all the partner's visual behaviour~\cite{anon2026brainhri}. Eye tracking and task performance complete the measured signals (part~2c).

\section{Current and Future Work: Model Adaptation}
\label{sec:current}
I ported VoiceChat to on-device inference and connected it to the testbed (Furhat speaks its audio; its tool calls act on the board), and replay corpus sessions with the model in one partner's seat. Steering (stage~2) was prototyped first, on the original model.

\subsection{Steering when to act (RQ1, current)}
Off the shelf, the model proposes speech onsets about 3.5$\times$ as often as the participant it replaces, yet matches only a quarter of their onsets (within $\pm$960\,ms), and zero-shot voice activity projection~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} scores below a one-bit previous-speaker rule. Prompt rewrites did not change when the model acts. Overrides after the argmax fail: an 85\% per-frame veto cut speech by only 35\% as the model re-proposes, and denying every proposal silenced it for good. Interventions before the argmax work: masking the tool-call token removed stray calls, and at sentence boundaries stopping is only 2--6 logits from winning. The hidden state also knows more than the head: a small recurrent head over $h_t$ predicts the partner's next onset with AUC 0.87 (leave-one-participant-out, 18 participants, under 400 labelled onsets), against 0.51 for the model's own start-versus-wait margin, doubling onset recall at the same false-start rate (37\% to 72\% at about five per minute). One study's data suffices to read the state; closed-loop evaluation comes next.

\subsection{Stage 1: task and gaze channels (RQ2)}
The model hears its partner but cannot see them or the board, or direct its own gaze. For the task, I built a JAX port of the game and trained a policy--value network, first by imitating the rule-based solver and then by AlphaZero-style self-play~\cite{silver2018alphazero} with Gumbel search~\cite{danihelka2022gumbel}: it wins 71\% of games at rulebook difficulty, where the solver wins 57\% on the easier setting used in the study. Its last hidden layer will enter the model as a task-state stream, and self-play supplies unlimited action targets. For gaze, I will add partner gaze as an input stream and a gaze head emitting discrete tokens alongside speech, as facial motion was added to full-duplex models~\cite{jiang2026moshiface} with finite scalar quantisation~\cite{mentzer2024fsq}, trained on task-specific gaze from the corpus and on conversational gaze data. In the corpus, the robot looking at the participant predicts their next onset a second ahead: gaze is a turn-yielding lever the robot controls. Fine-tuning changes $h_t$, so the steering head is then retrained, and gaze logits steered like speech logits.

\subsection{Stage 2: closing the loop (RQ3)}
The steering head now matches \emph{observed} behaviour; I will supervise it with \emph{self-reported} and \emph{measured} signals via two routes. In \emph{supervised learning}, predicted engagement, enjoyment, or task progress are targets that calibrate the bias per partner and moment. In \emph{reinforcement learning}, the bias is a low-dimensional policy over a few logits, optimised from these signals as rewards with sample-efficient methods such as contextual bandits, offline on the corpus and then online. The overrides finding sets a safety constraint: bias, but never deny every turn. Together, this offers HRI a recipe for adapting open full-duplex models to embodied collaboration, fine-tuning where data is abundant and steering with what a single study yields, and makes explicit the tension between matching human norms and serving \emph{this} partner.

\title{Steering When, Where, and Whether Robots Should Speak}
\subtitle{Data-Efficient Adapters for Full-Duplex Human--Robot Collaboration}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models have largely solved \emph{what} a robot should say; in embodied, task-oriented dialogue the harder questions are \emph{when}, \emph{where}, and \emph{whether} to say it. Full-duplex speech models listen and speak at once and can act through tool calls, yet off the shelf they talk too much, act too rarely, and cannot be retrained on the few interactions an HRI study yields. My research asks how LLM-based agents can gain the temporal, spatial, and social awareness that natural collaboration requires, from small data. I built a collaborative-game testbed with an aligned multimodal corpus, and supervision signals ranging from observed behaviour to self-reported enjoyment and measured brain activity. My current work steers a frozen, open full-duplex model by reading its hidden state and biasing its output logits; offline, this doubles turn-onset recall at a fixed false-start rate. Next, I will add gaze as an input and output channel and close the loop from human signals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have made robots fluent in \emph{what} to say. Natural collaboration also requires knowing \emph{when} to speak or act, \emph{where} to direct attention in the shared space, and \emph{whether} to speak at all given the partner's state. My thesis asks: \emph{How can we empower LLM-based agents with the social, temporal, and spatial awareness necessary to facilitate natural human--AI task-oriented embodied dialogue?}

The obstacle is data: an HRI study yields tens of participants and a few hours of interaction, orders of magnitude less than pretrained models learn from, and collaborative behaviours such as turn-taking~\cite{skantze2021turn} do not transfer for free. I therefore study \emph{steering}, adapting a frozen model through a small learned intervention instead of retraining it:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{RQ1 (temporal, \emph{when}):} Can a full-duplex model be steered, from one study's data, to take turns and act at human-like moments?
 \item \textbf{RQ2 (spatial, \emph{where}):} How can gaze, as input and output, ground dialogue in the shared task space?
 \item \textbf{RQ3 (social, \emph{whether}):} Which human signals (observed, self-reported, measured) should supervise steering so the agent adapts to its partner?
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Full-duplex models for embodied dialogue}
Cascaded ASR--LLM--TTS pipelines are turn-based and time their turns with silence thresholds. Lee et al.~\cite{lee2026recipe} give a modern recipe for situated, embodied conversation that pairs a real-time multimodal LLM with tool calls, but build it on the commercial OpenAI Realtime API, whose internal decisions cannot be inspected or changed. Open full-duplex models~\cite{defossez2024moshi} make those decisions explicit. My base model, NVIDIA NemotronLabs VoiceChat~\cite{nvidia2026voicechat}, is an open 11B full-duplex model and the first open one with tool calling: every 80\,ms it emits a token on a text channel (wait, start, or stop speaking) and on a function channel (call a tool or not). \emph{When} and \emph{whether} to speak or act become single tokens whose logits, and the hidden state behind them, can be read and changed.

\subsection{Steering LLMs}
Interventions differ in where they write, trading data needs against precision. \emph{Context} (prompts, injected state) needs no data but shapes \emph{what} is said more than \emph{when}. \emph{Soft embeddings}~\cite{lester2021prompt,li2021prefix} are learned but static, and fade over a long interaction. \emph{Weight} updates~\cite{houlsby2019adapters,hu2022lora,ouyang2022rlhf,rafailov2023dpo} are expressive but need far more data than HRI provides. \emph{Activation} steering~\cite{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,zou2023repe,li2023iti} is data-efficient but shifts everything downstream, including content. \emph{Logit} steering~\cite{dathathri2020pplm,yang2021fudge,krause2021gedi} reweights the next-token distribution, and \emph{output} overrides replace the chosen token afterwards.

\subsection{Rationale: read the state, write the logits}
The decisions I want to change \emph{are} tokens, so biasing their logits is the most targeted intervention: the backbone stays frozen, the effect is bounded and auditable, and only a small readout must be learned. My adapter \emph{reads} the hidden state, which knows more than the output head (Sec.~\ref{sec:current}), and \emph{writes} only the turn-taking and action logits.

\section{Prior Work: Testbed and Supervision}

\subsection{Testbed and observed behaviour}
I co-developed an open platform in which a participant and a Furhat robot~\cite{al2012furhat} play the cooperative board game \emph{Pandemic} on a shared touchscreen, with an inspectable game engine and an LLM dialogue manager~\cite{anon2026platform}. With it I collected 70 interactions with 36 participants: word-level transcripts annotated for interaction failures, isolated audio, facial action units, head pose and gaze, aligned with game events, robot logs, and questionnaires~\cite{anon2026dataset}. This is the \emph{observed} reference steering should match (part~3a).

\subsection{Self-reported experience}
Self-reports are the ground truth for experience but arrive once, after the interaction. Speech-to-Joy predicts post-interaction enjoyment from frozen speech embeddings, correlating significantly with self-reports and outperforming an experienced HRI annotator and LLM baselines while running in real time~\cite{anon2025joy}: an online proxy for a self-reported objective (part~3b).

\subsection{Measured signals}
To track cognitive state continuously, I adapted a multimodal whole-brain fMRI foundation model to live conversation, improving held-out prediction by 15\% over a ridge encoder~\cite{anon2026brainconv}. In two human--robot fMRI corpora (70 participants), transfer between human and robot partners depended on multimodal dynamics, above all the partner's visual behaviour~\cite{anon2026brainhri}. Eye tracking and task performance complete the measured signals (part~3c).

\section{Current Work: Temporal Awareness (RQ1)}
\label{sec:current}

I ported VoiceChat to on-device inference and connected it to the testbed (Furhat speaks its audio; its tool calls act on the board), and replay corpus sessions with the model in one partner's seat.

\subsection{Off-the-shelf behaviour is misaligned}
The model proposes speech onsets about 3.5$\times$ as often as the participant it replaces, yet matches only a quarter of their onsets (within $\pm$960\,ms). Zero-shot voice activity projection~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} does not close the gap, scoring below a one-bit previous-speaker rule (macro-F1 0.58 vs.\ 0.76).

\subsection{Where to intervene}
Five prompt rewrites did not change when the model acts; restructuring its \emph{input} did, as pushing the board state as tool responses and a propose-then-act turn raised a scenario suite from 5/14 to 10/15. Overrides after the argmax fail: an 85\% per-frame veto cut speech by only 35\% because the model re-proposes, and denying every proposal silenced it for good within 2--3 minutes. Interventions before the argmax work: masking the tool-call token removed stray calls entirely, and at sentence boundaries stopping is only 2--6 logits from winning.

\subsection{The hidden state knows more than the head}
A small recurrent readout over $h_t$ predicts the partner's next onset with AUC 0.87 (leave-one-participant-out, 18 participants, under 400 labelled onsets), against 0.51 for the model's own start-versus-wait margin, doubling onset recall at the same false-start rate (37\% to 72\% at about five per minute). One study's data suffices to read the state. These results are offline, with the model in the participant's seat; the robot's seat and closed-loop evaluation come next.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\subsection{Spatial awareness: a gaze channel (RQ2)}
The model hears its partner but cannot see them or direct its own gaze. I will add partner gaze as an input stream and a gaze head emitting discrete tokens alongside speech, as facial motion was added to full-duplex models~\cite{jiang2026moshiface} with finite scalar quantisation~\cite{mentzer2024fsq}. In the corpus, the robot looking at the participant predicts their next onset a second ahead, so gaze is a turn-yielding lever the robot controls, and the brain results single out the partner's visual behaviour. Gaze logits can then be steered like speech logits.

\subsection{Social awareness: closing the loop (RQ3)}
The readout now matches \emph{observed} behaviour; I will supervise it with \emph{self-reported} and \emph{measured} signals via two routes. In \emph{supervised learning}, predicted engagement, enjoyment, or task progress are targets that calibrate the bias per partner and moment. In \emph{reinforcement learning}, the bias is a low-dimensional policy over a few logits, optimised from these signals as rewards with sample-efficient methods (contextual bandits, policy gradients), offline on the corpus and then online, far cheaper than RLHF or DPO on the weights~\cite{ouyang2022rlhf,rafailov2023dpo}. The overrides finding sets a safety constraint: bias, but never deny every turn. Together, this work offers HRI a recipe for adapting open full-duplex models to embodied collaboration with the data a single study produces, and makes explicit the tension between matching human norms and serving \emph{this} partner.
